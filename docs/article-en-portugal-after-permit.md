## Article

Every guide to moving to Portugal ends at the same place: the permit is granted, and the story stops. What actually follows is a decade — renewals on one clock, a card on another, a permanent status at five years, citizenship at seven or ten. This page covers that decade, and quotes the article of law behind each rule.

### What the Portuguese residence card is, and what expires when

The document you receive is the **título de residência**. It is not the permit. The permit is the legal authorisation; the card is the object that proves you hold it, and the two run on different clocks.

That distinction is the single most useful thing on this page, because it answers a question people ask in the wrong form. "When does my permanent residence expire?" has no answer, and here is why. **Article 76(1) of Lei 23/2007:**

> "A autorização de residência permanente não tem limite de validade."

The permanent authorisation has no limit of validity. It does not expire. What expires is the card, and it is renewed every five years — a renewal of the document, not a re-application for the status behind it.

Before that stage, the card and the permit expire together, on the schedule in the next section.

One thing we are not going to tell you, and the omission is deliberate. Whether the card serves as identification inside Portugal is governed by article 84 of the same law, and today that article could not be read in any primary source: AIMA's own portal returns a certificate error, Diário da República is behind a JavaScript gate, and the PGD Lisboa copy of the law truncates before it. Rather than repeat what other pages assert about it, we leave it out until it can be quoted.

### How long the residence card takes after approval: nobody publishes it

Search for this and you will find confident numbers. None of them cites anything.

What can be established is the clock on the decision, not on the card. The law gives AIMA **60 days** to decide a grant and 30 to decide a renewal, under article 82 of Lei 23/2007. In practice the wait runs from **one to three years**, and on **4 August 2026** the agency had roughly **30 000 undecided cases**.

For the step after the decision — printing and handing over the card — **no deadline exists in the statute, and AIMA does not publish one.** That is the honest answer, and it is worth more than an invented range: if a firm tells you the card takes a specific number of weeks, ask which instrument sets it.

### Renewing a Portuguese residence permit: two years, then three

**Article 75(1)** sets the rhythm:

> "a autorização de residência temporária é válida pelo período de dois anos contados a partir da data da emissão do respetivo título e é renovável por períodos sucessivos de três anos."

Two years from the issue of the title, then successive three-year periods. Note where the clock starts — the **issue of the title**, not the date of the decision and not the date you arrived.

The fees are published and do not change with the route: **842,80 €** to have the application examined, **8 418,90 €** for the grant, **4 210,30 €** for a renewal, with **25 % off** when the submission is made online.

A caution about the wording of this rule. The current text of article 75(1) was reachable only in a secondary compilation; the primary confirmation that it stands is Lei 61/2025, which re-enacts article 75 and rewrites only its second paragraph. So the rule is current — but do not repeat the claim, common online, that the periods "changed from one plus two to two plus three" on a particular date. The instrument that would have made that change could not be identified.

### Permanent residency in Portugal: what article 80 actually requires

Five years of temporary residence is the condition people know. It is one of five, and they are cumulative — all must hold. **Article 80(1)** lists them:

| Requirement | What the statute says |
|---|---|
| Time held | Temporary residence permit for at least five years |
| Criminal record | No convictions in the last five years exceeding one year of imprisonment, alone or combined |
| Means | Means of subsistence, as defined by the portaria referred to in article 52(1)(d) |
| Housing | Accommodation |
| Language | Proof of knowledge of basic Portuguese |

Two of these rows are where the market is wrong.

**The income test does not go away.** Point (c) applies the same means-of-subsistence standard at the five-year mark that applied at the start. A reader who assumed the financial question was settled when the first permit was granted meets it again here.

**The language requirement in the statute is not A2.** Article 80(1)(e) says "conhecimento do Português básico" — knowledge of basic Portuguese — and stops. The A2 equivalence that every page publishes is set at regulation level, below the statute. It may well be what you are asked for in practice; it is not what the law says, and a page that presents it as statutory is quoting something it has not read.

Time already spent in the country before the law came into force counts, under article 80(2).

### EU long-term resident status is a different status, and the law says so

This is the confusion worth clearing, because two statuses sit side by side and most guides collapse them into one.

Article 80(1) opens with a subordinating clause:

> "Sem prejuízo das disposições da presente lei relativas ao estatuto dos nacionais de Estados terceiros residentes de longa duração…"

Without prejudice to the provisions of this law on the status of third-country nationals who are long-term residents. In other words, the statute expressly sets that regime aside before describing this one. National permanent residence under article 80 and EU long-term resident status — the Portuguese transposition of Directive 2003/109/EC — are parallel routes, not two names for the same thing.

![2]

Both ask for five years of lawful residence. They differ in what they are for: the national status secures your position in Portugal, the EU status is designed to travel, carrying rights of residence in other member states subject to their conditions. Which one serves you depends on whether Portugal is the destination or the door.

### And then citizenship

Naturalisation is the end of this chain and a subject of its own — the 2026 law changed the qualifying period to ten years for most applicants and seven for citizens of EU and Portuguese-speaking countries. The arithmetic, the transitional rule and what counts towards the clock are set out in our guide to the D7 visa and Portuguese residency.

One point belongs here rather than there: what accumulates towards naturalisation is **days actually lived in Portugal**, not years of holding a permit. A permit renewed from abroad keeps your status and builds nothing.

### Frequently asked questions

**When does permanent residence in Portugal expire?**
It does not. Article 76(1) states that the permanent authorisation has no limit of validity. The card is renewed every five years; the status behind it is not re-applied for.

**How often do I renew a Portuguese residence permit?**
The first title runs two years from its date of issue, then renewals run three years each, under article 75(1).

**How long does the residence card take to arrive after approval?**
No statute sets a deadline for issuing the card, and AIMA publishes none. The law gives 60 days for a decision on a grant; in practice that stage runs one to three years, with about 30 000 cases undecided as of 4 August 2026.

**What does permanent residency in Portugal cost?**
The published fees are 842,80 € to examine the application, 8 418,90 € for a grant and 4 210,30 € for a renewal, reduced by 25 % for online submission.

**Do I need A2 Portuguese for permanent residence?**
The statute says "basic Portuguese" and specifies no level. A2 comes from regulation, not from article 80.

**Is EU long-term resident status the same as Portuguese permanent residence?**
No. Article 80 explicitly sets the long-term resident regime aside before setting out its own conditions. They are parallel statuses with different purposes.
